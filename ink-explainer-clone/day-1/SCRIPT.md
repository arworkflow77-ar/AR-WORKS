# WHEN DID HUMANS START KISSING? AND WHY?

## COLD OPEN

You probably did it this morning. Or last night. You probably didn't think about it at all. You leaned in, tilted your head to the right — you didn't choose the direction, it chose itself — you closed your eyes, and for three, maybe five seconds, the most sensitive square centimeters of your entire body pressed against the most sensitive square centimeters of somebody else's.

[ON-SCREEN: RIGHT NOW]

And then it was over, and you went back to your day, and the moment was gone. But here's what you just did without thinking about it. You performed an act that a Babylonian scribe once had to invent a verb for, that a Roman needed three different words to describe, that a Roman poet wrote a poem about just to count it. And none of them were the beginning either. Because the kiss is older than writing, older than cities, older than us.

[ON-SCREEN: 4,500 YEARS AGO — NOT THE BEGINNING]

The oldest written kiss ever found is 4,500 years old. And when archaeologists finally read it, here's the strange part. The kiss in that text wasn't new. It was already normal. Which means the real question isn't when humans started kissing. It's how an act this strange became so normal that a caveman's grandchild does it without thinking. So let's find the first kiss. And I mean the actual first one.

[ON-SCREEN: THE FIRST KISS]

## PART 1 — THE KISS THAT ISN'T OURS

To find it, we have to do something uncomfortable. We have to stop believing the kiss is about us. Because your brain has a story ready. Kissing is natural. Kissing is universal. Every human, everywhere, has always done it. That's what everyone believes. For centuries, that was the official story. But the proof that it's wrong was sitting in a rainforest the entire time. And when scientists finally watched closely enough, it flipped everything upside down.

Here's the scene. Two chimpanzees have just fought. There was screaming, chasing, fur flying. And now, minutes later, the loser is sitting a few meters away, hunched, glancing sideways. The winner watches him. Nobody moves. And then, the winner walks over, leans in, and kisses him. Right on the mouth. And the fight is over.

[ON-SCREEN: KISS = PEACE]

Primatologists have watched this happen for decades. For chimpanzees, a kiss is not romance. It's a peace treaty. After a conflict, chimps kiss and embrace to say: the fight is finished, we are still allies. It happens more between males than between females, which tells you it's politics, not passion. Their cousins, the bonobos, go further — bonobos kiss with open mouths and tongues, and they do it constantly, for greeting, for comfort, for food, for nothing at all. The bonobo uses the kiss the way we use a handshake, an apology, and a hug at the same time.

So the kiss did not begin with lovers. It began with enemies becoming friends again. But that still doesn't explain how a peace gesture between apes became the thing you do with your eyes closed and your heart racing. For that, there's a wilder theory. And it's beautiful.

[ON-SCREEN: THE GROOMER'S FINAL KISS]

In 2024, an evolutionary psychologist named Adriano Lameira at the University of Warwick published a hypothesis with a perfect name: the groomer's final kiss. Here's the logic. For millions of years, our ancestors lived in fur. And fur means parasites. So apes groom each other for hours — fingers combing, picking, clearing. Grooming is not just hygiene. It's the currency of trust. It's how apes say, you matter to me, I will take care of you. But here's the part nobody tells you. A grooming session has a final step. When the fingers have done their work, the groomer leans in with pursed, protruding lips and gives one last, light suction against the skin — the last tick, the last bit of debris. The final kiss.

[ON-SCREEN: FINGERS FIRST. THEN LIPS.]

Now watch what happens next, because it's the entire history of kissing in one move. Our ancestors slowly lost their fur. And as the fur thinned, grooming got shorter. There was less to pick, less to clean. But the final step never disappeared. The last kiss stayed. Long after the fur was gone and the parasites were gone and the grooming itself was gone, two apes were still ending their ritual the same way — lips, pressure, a moment of trust. The hygiene vanished. The signal remained. Lameira calls the kiss a behavioral fossil — a crystallized relic of grooming, the last surviving frame of a ritual that once took hours. You are not performing a romantic act when you kiss. You are performing the end of a grooming session between animals that no longer have fur.

There's a competing theory, and you should hear it. Some scientists argue the kiss comes from mothers, not groomers. In the early years of human evolution, mothers chewed food for their babies and passed it mouth-to-mouth. Premastication, it's called. Watch a mother bird feeding her chicks and you've seen the shape of it. The idea is that the kiss is a leftover of being fed — warmth, lips, nourishment, safety, all arriving together at the mouth. Lameira pushes back: in a real kiss, nobody is passing food, and the suction direction is wrong. But both theories agree on the one thing that matters. The kiss began as care. Grooming or feeding — either way, the first kiss was somebody leaning close to somebody they wanted to keep safe.

[ON-SCREEN: CARE FIRST. ROMANCE LATER.]

So by the time humans appear, the mouth-to-skin kiss is already ancient. The question is when two mouths started finding each other on purpose. And for that, we finally get to read something. Because around 2,500 years before Christ, between two rivers, people started writing things down — and they wrote about kissing.

## PART 2 — THE FIRST WRITTEN KISS

[ON-SCREEN: MESOPOTAMIA · 2500 BCE]

The place is Mesopotamia, the land between the Euphrates and the Tigris, in what is now Iraq and Syria. The people there pressed wedge-shaped marks into wet clay with a reed. Cuneiform. And those tablets survived by the tens of thousands — contracts, tax records, prayers, complaints, recipes. And, buried in the middle of all that bureaucracy, kisses.

In 2023, two researchers — Troels Pank Arbøll of the University of Copenhagen and Sophie Lund Rasmussen of Oxford — went through those tablets and published what they found in the journal Science. Kissing was already a well-established practice 4,500 years ago. Not new, not exotic, not scandalous. Established. Married couples kissed, usually in the context of sex, paying attention to each other's lips specifically. Unmarried lovers kissed too — more rarely, and more dangerously, because there were rules about that sort of thing. And the gods kissed. The oldest references to kissing are in mythological texts — the gods of Mesopotamia spent a surprising amount of time embracing and kissing, which tells you what the humans writing about them considered normal.

But the two finds that get under your skin are not about gods.

The first is a love poem. It was written in the city of Sippar, roughly 3,800 years ago, in Akkadian, and it contains a line so direct it needs no translation. "My upper lip becomes moist, while my lower lip trembles! I shall embrace him, I shall kiss him."

[ON-SCREEN: "MY UPPER LIP BECOMES MOIST…"]

Read that again. A wet upper lip. A trembling lower lip. That is not a poet inventing a metaphor. That is someone who has been kissed writing down exactly what it does to a body. The physical detail is the proof. Kissing was common enough that a 3,800-year-old poet could describe its mechanics and expect everyone to recognize it.

And the second find is stranger. It's a legal document from the city of Larsa. A woman named Shat-Marduk has been ordered to cut all contact with a man — and the tablet records her oath, word for word. She swears he shall not return to her, he shall not propose, and then the tablet gets specific. "He shall not kiss my lips." Think about that for a second. In the year 1900 BC, kissing was intimate enough that it belonged in a legally binding oath, and common enough that everyone in the courtroom knew exactly what the clause meant. The kiss was already so woven into life that law had to mention it.

[ON-SCREEN: LARSA · "HE SHALL NOT KISS MY LIPS"]

And then there's the dark side, because the tablets record that too. Mesopotamian medical texts describe a disease called bu'shanu — sores and vesicles around the mouth and throat, symptoms that match herpes simplex virus 1, the cold-sore virus, which today infects most of humanity and spreads, among other ways, by kissing. Arbøll and Rasmussen are careful here — you can't diagnose a 4,000-year-old disease from a clay tablet. But the parallel is hard to ignore. The kiss had a cost. And here's the twist their research really lands. For decades, the standard story was that kissing was invented once — in a single bronze-age civilization, around 1500 BC, and from there it spread across the world, and somewhere along that journey it also spread a herpes strain. The Mesopotamian tablets, a thousand years older, destroy that story. Kissing wasn't invented in one place and exported. It appears in Mesopotamia, Egypt, and the Mediterranean in parallel — the same act, arrived at independently, in cultures that had no contact. Which means the kiss wasn't a trend. It was something cultures kept inventing because it worked.

[ON-SCREEN: NOT ONE ORIGIN. MANY.]

So the written record gives us the kiss as law, as poetry, as disease. Now watch how fast it becomes a science. Because one civilization decided the kiss deserved a vocabulary of its own.

## PART 3 — THE KISS BECOMES A SYSTEM

Start in Rome. The Romans had a word problem. Latin had one word for a kiss, and reality kept demanding more. So they ended up with three. Osculum — the respectful kiss, given on the cheek, between friends, family, officials. Basium — the kiss of affection on the lips. And savium — the lover's kiss, the deep one, the one that belonged in bedrooms and poems. A Roman playwright named Plautus has a character beg for "savia super savia" — kiss upon kiss upon kiss. When a language splits one act into three words, the act has become load-bearing. It holds different weights in different rooms. A Roman politician kissing a colleague's cheek at the forum and a Roman poet kissing a lover at midnight were, linguistically, doing two different things.

[ON-SCREEN: OSCULUM | BASIUM | SAVIUM]

But the Romans didn't stop at words. They wrote instruction manuals. Around the year 2 BC, the poet Ovid published the Ars Amatoria — the Art of Love. Three books. A full manual of seduction that reads like a textbook, and it is packed with kissing. When to take a kiss. How to take it. How to make sure she remembers it. A few years later, the emperor Augustus banished Ovid to the far edge of the empire — and some historians still argue that very manual was part of the reason.

[ON-SCREEN: ARS AMATORIA · 2 BCE]

A civilization writing step-by-step instructions for a kiss is a civilization that has thought about the kiss more than you ever will. And then there's Catullus. Around the same century, the poet Catullus wrote the most famous kiss poem in Western history. Five lines. Basically math. Give me a thousand kisses, he tells his lover. Then a hundred. Then another thousand. Then a second hundred. Then, when we've kissed many thousands, let us lose count — so that no one can envy us, knowing how many kisses there were.

[ON-SCREEN: BASIA MILLE — A THOUSAND KISSES]

He was counting something uncountable, and he knew the number was never the point. So now we have the kiss as ape politics, the kiss as clay-tablet law, the kiss as Roman vocabulary, the kiss as Roman math. And at this point, you might be thinking — fine, it's ancient, it's everywhere, it's universal, case closed. But that's exactly the moment the story turns. Because there's a study. And it found something nobody expected.

[ON-SCREEN: WAIT TILL YOU HEAR THIS.]

## PART 4 — THE KISS GOES PUBLIC

So the kiss is old. But watch what happens when it meets crowds, because this is where it nearly gets itself banned. And if you want to understand why people get so emotional about kissing — why your parents looked away, why a screen kiss makes a theater go quiet — this is the part of the story that explains it.

[ON-SCREEN: 16 JULY 1439]

England, 1439. Plague is moving through the country again, and the king does something that sounds like a joke but is completely real. Henry the Sixth officially bans kissing. A royal proclamation, dated the 16th of July, forbids the practice to stop the spread of pestilence. And before you laugh, understand the logic. In the 15th century, kissing was not a private romantic act. It was infrastructure. Knights kissed the king on the mouth as an oath of loyalty. Parishioners kissed each other in greeting. Servants kissed their master's ring. A medieval court ran on kisses the way a modern office runs on handshakes — and if you wanted to protect the king from a disease, you had to shut the whole system down. The ban did not last, of course. It dissolved, in the words of one chronicler, into lip-service. But the fact that a government once tried to ban kissing tells you how public the act used to be.

Now jump four centuries, to a theater in New York. It's 1896, and Thomas Edison's studio has just made an 18-second film called, simply, The Kiss. It shows a man and a woman — actors from a stage musical — nuzzling, then kissing on the lips. Eighteen seconds. Close-up.

[ON-SCREEN: 1896 · THE KISS · 18 SECONDS]

The first kiss ever captured on film almost ended film. Newspapers called it "absolutely disgusting." One critic wrote that the spectacle of the prolonged pasturing on each other's lips was beastly enough on stage, but magnified to gargantuan proportions it was worse. The Catholic Church demanded censorship. Cities threatened police action against theaters that showed it. An 18-second peck on the lips, in 1896, was treated the way we'd treat something genuinely dangerous. Because this is the pattern, and it runs through the entire history of the kiss. Every time the kiss becomes visible to too many people at once, someone panics. The private kiss is poetry. The public kiss is a scandal.

And then, the last twist in this story — the name. You know the term. The French kiss. Here's what nobody tells you about it. The French did not invent it. They didn't even have a word for it. Roman poets were writing manuals and love poems about kissing twenty centuries before anyone called it French. The term was invented by American and British soldiers returning from France after the First World War — they'd watched French couples kiss openly, in the street, in daylight, the way their own countries reserved for locked doors, and they brought the label home with them. The French themselves had to borrow the concept back. As late as 2014, the dictionary Le Petit Robert officially added a verb for it — galocher, to kiss with tongues — because the language that supposedly invented the thing never bothered to name it. The kiss has never been about nations. It's just that every nation that sees it done openly assumes some other nation must have started it.

[ON-SCREEN: "FRENCH KISS" — INVENTED BY AMERICAN SOLDIERS · 2014: GALOCHER]

## PART 5 — THE KISS THAT NEVER HAPPENED

In 2015, three anthropologists — William Jankowiak, Shelly Volsche, and Justin Garcia — decided to test the most basic assumption in the entire history of this subject. The assumption that the romantic kiss is a human universal. They took a database of world cultures, 168 of them, and they checked each one for a simple thing. Does this culture kiss romantically? Lip to lip, as part of love or sex?

[ON-SCREEN: 168 CULTURES. ONE QUESTION.]

Here's what they found. The romantic-sexual kiss is not universal. It's not even close to universal. It appears in 46 percent of the cultures they examined. Less than half. In the other 54 percent, people fall in love, form couples, raise children — and never once kiss on the mouth.

[ON-SCREEN: 46%]

And it gets sharper. In 1890, in South Africa, the Thonga people watched Europeans kissing for the first time. Their reaction is recorded. They found it unclean and unpleasant. Quote, roughly: these people eat each other's saliva. Now, you laughed at that. But here's the thing nobody tells you. There are cultures right now, on this planet, where your goodnight kiss looks exactly like that — like two people swapping spit, and not in a poetic way. The Mehinaku of Brazil, the Hadza of Tanzania — ethnographers recorded that romantic kissing was simply absent, or actively mocked. The kiss you think of as the most natural thing a human can do is, to a large portion of humanity, a foreign custom, like eating with chopsticks or kissing cheeks three times. It's culture. It's learned.

[ON-SCREEN: NOT UNIVERSAL. NOT NATURAL. LEARNED.]

And then the study reveals the pattern hiding inside the numbers, and this is the real payoff. The kiss correlates with social complexity. The more stratified, the more urban, the more organized a society is, the more likely its people are to kiss romantically. Hunter-gatherers rarely do. Big agricultural civilizations do. Why would that be? The researchers don't claim to fully know, but think about what a kiss demands. It demands privacy. It demands leisure. It demands that two people can be alone long enough for a slow, useless, beautiful act. In a small nomadic band, life is lived in public — and the mouth is for eating, for talking, for surviving. The kiss is a luxury good. And like most luxury goods, it was invented by the people who had room for it.

So the kiss was never written into our biology as romance. It was a peace gesture between apes that some cultures kept and most cultures didn't. Which raises the question you came here with, the one under all of this. If kissing isn't universal, if it's optional, if half of humanity never does it — then why does it feel like gravity to you? Why does a kiss rearrange your entire nervous system?

[ON-SCREEN: WHY DOES IT FEEL LIKE GRAVITY?]

Because of what it does to your body. And here's where the science gets almost unfair.

## PART 6 — THE CHEMISTRY OF A KISS

Your lips are a scandal of engineering. They are packed with nerve endings — denser than your fingertips — and they don't have the protection your fingertips have. They're thin-skinned, wet, warm, and wired directly into the oldest, most emotional parts of your brain. When lips meet, your body gets flooded with information at a bandwidth nothing else matches. Taste, texture, temperature, breath, pressure, motion — all of it arriving at once. Scientists have a word for the study of kissing. Philematology. It's real. There are researchers whose entire career is this moment.

[ON-SCREEN: PHILEMATOLOGY]

And the chemistry is the part that explains why you keep doing it. A real kiss triggers a cascade. Dopamine — the reward chemical, the same one involved in addiction — spikes, which is why a good kiss feels like winning something. Oxytocin — the bonding hormone — rises, which is why couples who kiss more report feeling closer. Stress hormones drop. Heart rates sync up between partners. A long kiss is, chemically speaking, a small, portable bonding ritual that works on two nervous systems at once.

[ON-SCREEN: DOPAMINE ↑ OXYTOCIN ↑ STRESS ↓]

But here's my favorite detail in the entire story, and I saved it for last. In 2003, a German neuroscientist named Onur Güntürkün published a study in Nature. He and his team stood in airports, train stations, beaches, and parks — in the United States, Germany, and Turkey — and watched 124 couples kiss. They weren't looking at the kissing. They were looking at the head tilt. Which way does the head go when two people kiss?

[ON-SCREEN: WHICH WAY DOES YOUR HEAD TURN?]

Twice as many people turn their heads to the right as to the left. About 65 percent right, 35 percent left. You've done this your whole life and never noticed that you have a side. But here's why the number matters. Babies, in the final weeks in the womb, and in their first months outside it, show the exact same bias — they tend to turn their heads to the right. It's one of the earliest behavioral asymmetries a human develops. Güntürkün's argument is that your kissing direction is a fossil too — a motor preference laid down before you were born, surviving untouched into your adult life. Think about what that means. When you lean in for a kiss, the tilt is older than your first memory, older than your first word, older than you. You made the decision before you had a name.

[ON-SCREEN: 65% RIGHT · 35% LEFT]

## THE ENDING

So here's where the story actually lands. And remember the morning you started with. You kissed someone. You didn't think. You tilted right, because a version of you from before you were born decided that. Your lips did what lips evolved to do — they opened a channel between two nervous systems. And inside that channel was everything. The end of a grooming session between furred apes, millions of years back. A peace treaty between two chimpanzees who chose allies over enemies. A trembling lower lip pressed into clay in a city called Sippar, 3,800 years ago. A woman in Larsa swearing under oath that a man would never kiss her lips again. A Roman with three words for one act. A poet counting kisses by the thousand. A German neuroscientist counting head tilts in an airport. And the 46 percent — the half of humanity that watched all of this and said, no thanks, we'll do something else with our mouths.

[ON-SCREEN: EVERYTHING IS IN THE KISS]

The kiss is not universal, and that's the point of it. It's optional, which means every culture that kisses chose to keep it. It's useless, which means it survived because nothing useful could replace it. It's a fossil, which means when you kiss someone you're holding the last fragment of a ritual from before we were human. The groomer's final kiss. The last step of a grooming session that no longer exists, between two bodies that no longer have fur. You lean in. You close your eyes. You tilt right. And for three seconds, you are ten million years old.

And that's why nobody can remember their first kiss properly. Because it wasn't theirs. It was the end of an ancient argument between two animals, deciding — for one more generation — to stay friends.
